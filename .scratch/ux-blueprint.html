UX/UI Foundation · Pre-Screen Design Blueprint

Solar Site Fitness & Capacity Engine

A design blueprint for the interface layer of a decision-support engine that answers, for any candidate rooftop or water body: can solar be installed here, and at what capacity — with the evidence to defend that answer.

Source Development Document v1.1, rev. 24 Aug 2026 Scope Product understanding → screen-by-screen readiness Status Foundation — no screens designed yet
01

Understand the Product

What it is, who it serves, what it must never get wrong

The engine answers two questions about a site — can solar go here, and at what capacity — and returns a verdict with a number, a confidence level, and the one constraint that produced that number. Everything the interface does is in service of making that answer fast to get and easy to trust.

Five external buyers sit around one engine: a state renewable-energy agency screening thousands of water bodies for floating solar; the same agency triaging tens of thousands of government rooftops; a RESCO bid team pricing a portfolio it has never visited; a DISCOM programme cell targeting rooftop subsidy outreach by feeder; and a corporate sustainability head building an audit-ready site portfolio. A sixth persona is internal but load-bearing: the field/geometry operator who draws boundaries by hand, confirms OCR-extracted data, and reconciles survey results against the model.

What must feel effortless vs. what must ask for confirmation

Effortless

Entering an address or dropping a pin and getting a verdict back; re-selecting a site someone else already analysed (served from cache, instantly); scanning a portfolio table for the sites worth a second look; reading why a number is what it is without opening a manual.

Requires confirmation / deliberate weight

Promoting a GPT‑4 Vision boundary suggestion over the provider's original geometry; accepting OCR‑extracted USN data before it's stored; forcing a cache refresh on a location everyone else is relying on; publishing a recalibrated utilisation factor or a new ML model version; any bulk recompute across a jurisdiction.

What a user must understand in the first few seconds

  • This is a pre-feasibility instrument, not an approval — every result carries that limitation visibly, not in a footnote.
  • A verdict is never a bare number. It always arrives with confidence and the one constraint holding it back.
  • Whether they're looking at a single site or a portfolio of forty thousand, the same four facts anchor the screen: capacity, verdict, confidence, binding constraint.

Requirement classification

Separating what the document states from what follows from it keeps the design honest about where it is inventing.

Explicit in the document
  • Single response shape across all site types (API‑01)
  • Verdict set: SUITABLE / SUITABLE_SUBJECT_TO_SURVEY / CONDITIONAL / INSUFFICIENT_DATA / NOT_SUITABLE
  • Confidence, binding constraint and reason list on every result (FIT‑04–06)
  • ML score is additive, never authoritative (FIT‑06, ML‑01)
  • Cache hits must be visibly labelled, never disguised (CACHE‑05)
  • Async batch jobs with pollable status (API‑03)
  • USN capture only on billing‑linked rooftop types, three converging input paths (USN‑01–05)
Strong UX conclusions
  • The interface is a workspace tool, used repeatedly by professionals — not a marketing funnel
  • Map and evidence panel must coexist in one view; verdicts detached from geography lose trust
  • Confidence needs a consistent visual language reused everywhere, not a tooltip
  • Portfolio scale (hundreds–tens of thousands of sites) must be the default screen, not an edge case
  • Boundary review (VIS/VIZ) is a judged human decision, so it needs its own deliberate screen, not an inline diff
Assumptions (flag before building)
  • Most analysts work primarily on desktop/laptop; field operators need a lightweight mobile mode
  • Tenants see only their own sites, but benefit anonymously from the shared cache
  • Registry/dashboard/survey workflow screens exist as noted in the spec but are out of this FRD's scope — assumed present in v1.0 packaging
  • Government buyers expect exportable, offline-shareable artefacts (PDF/CSV) alongside the live product
Open questions
  • Who approves a promoted VIS boundary or a new ML model version — a named role, or any operator?
  • Does a tenant ever see that a result came from another tenant's earlier analysis, or is provenance fully hidden?
  • What does "force refresh" require procedurally — reason code, approval, audit log surfaced in UI?
  • Weather API provider is still open (§10.3) — does its latency profile affect the "instant" promise?
02

UX Strategy

The principles every screen answers to before any screen exists

Product hierarchy

Engine → Site → Analysis → Assessment → Portfolio. A site is a place; an analysis is what the engine computed at that location (and may be shared across tenants via the cache); an assessment is a tenant's dated, versioned view of that analysis attached to their site record; a portfolio is a ranked collection of assessments. The interface must keep these four nouns visually distinct — conflating "site" and "assessment" is the single most likely source of confusion once caching and re-analysis enter the picture.

Information hierarchy — the constant, at every zoom level

1 · Verdict

The word — SUITABLE, CONDITIONAL, etc. Read before anything else.

2 · Capacity

The number, in kWp DC, with its unit basis always explicit.

3 · Confidence

How much to trust the number, expressed the same way everywhere.

4 · Binding constraint

The one thing holding the number back — the fact a user will act on.

Everything else — the full ceiling list, generation P50/P90, the ML score, the panorama, imagery date — is supporting evidence one level down. Nothing at that second level competes visually with the four facts above it.

Navigation model

A persistent workspace shell (left sidebar + top utility bar), not a marketing-style top nav. Primary sections are nouns a professional already thinks in: Portfolio, Sites, Imports/Jobs, Reports, Configuration. The map is not a section — it is a mode available from Portfolio and from any Site. Search is global and geography-aware: typing an address, a USN, or a site name all resolve the same way.

User mental model

Every persona is really doing one thing: spending a limited survey/decision budget on the right subset of sites. The product's job is to make that subset obvious. This reframes "dashboard" away from vanity metrics and toward a single question the home screen must answer immediately: which sites, out of everything I have, deserve my next hour?

Primary vs. secondary actions

ContextPrimary actionSecondary actions

Progressive disclosure strategy

  1. Glance — verdict, capacity, confidence, binding constraint. This is the entire portfolio table row and the entire assessment card header.
  2. Scan — the reason list, the ceiling breakdown, generation estimate, imagery/geometry provenance. One click or one panel away.
  3. Inspect — the full ceiling ledger, ML score with model version, cache lineage, vision-refinement annotation, panorama, raw geometry. Reached deliberately, never defaulted into.
  4. Audit — parameter pack version, constraint pack version, historical reproduction of a past result. For configuration and compliance roles, not day-to-day analysts.

Where users will get confused, and the counter-move

ML score vs. FIT verdict

Two numbers that both sound like "the answer." Counter-move: the ML score never appears in the same visual weight or position as the verdict — it lives in a clearly labelled "model comparison" panel, always with a one-line reminder of which one is authoritative.

Cache reuse feeling like a shortcut was taken

An instant result can read as "it didn't really check." Counter-move: make the cache hit a visible, positive state — "Already analysed on <date> · reused" — not a hidden optimisation.

Site types with different field sets (USN only on rooftop, minimum-spread only on floating)

Counter-move: forms and detail views are generated from the same type registry the backend validates against — a field that doesn't apply to this site type is never shown greyed out, it's simply absent.

Async batch jobs disappearing from view

A 2,000-site bid-window import must never feel like it vanished into a queue. Counter-move: a persistent, always-reachable Jobs surface with live progress, not a one-time toast.

Reducing cognitive load

  • One consistent verdict/confidence visual language, reused identically in the table row, the card, the map pin, and the PDF export.
  • Numbers are never shown without their unit and basis (kWp DC, m², P50/P90) — inconsistency here is where trust erodes fastest.
  • Filters and rankings default to the decision the persona is there to make (e.g. floating inventory defaults to sorted-by-MW-descending, not alphabetical).
  • Every INSUFFICIENT_DATA state names exactly what is missing — never a bare "no data" dead end.

06

UI Design Direction

A visual language earned by the subject: survey precision, not startup gloss

The product sits closer to a surveying instrument than a marketing app — it is used by people who need to defend a number to a tender committee or an auditor. The direction is technical, calm and evidentiary: precise, quiet, and built to make data trustworthy at a glance, never decorative for its own sake.

Precision

Hairline rules, tabular numerals, exact alignment — the visual grammar of a survey table, not a dashboard template.

Restraint

One accent, used sparingly for action and emphasis. Verdict color is semantic and kept separate from brand color.

Evidentiary

Every figure is traceable — source, date, version sit one glance away, never hidden behind a hover.

Color strategy

A cool, low-saturation neutral base — paper, not chrome — carries the density of tables and maps without fatigue. A single warm accent (solar amber) marks action and emphasis, kept deliberately apart from the cooler structural blue used for wayfinding and labels. Verdict and status colors are a separate semantic palette so a user never confuses "this is clickable" with "this is suitable."

Paper#F5F4EE — base ground
Surface#EAE8DE — cards, table zebra
Ink#1E2B30 — primary text
Structural blue#2C5C6B — labels, links, wayfinding
Solar amber#A9691F — the one accent, actions & emphasis
Line#C7C1AF — hairline borders

Semantic verdict palette — deliberately distinct from the accent above

SUITABLE

Deep, low-chroma green. Reserved for verdict states only — never reused as a UI accent.

CONDITIONAL

Warm amber-brown, close enough to the brand accent to read as "attention" but distinguishable in context and never used for buttons.

NOT SUITABLE

Muted brick red — never pure red. Paired with an icon, never color alone.

INSUFFICIENT_DATA takes the neutral grey chip above, deliberately un-alarming — it is a data gap, not a bad outcome, and must never visually read as a low score (per FIT‑03 / non‑negotiables).

Typography

For the product (distinct from this document, which is set in a reading-optimised serif): IBM Plex Sans for interface and headings, and IBM Plex Mono for every place a number, coordinate, ID or code needs to line up and be trusted — table figures, capacity values, requirement/version tags, coordinates. The Plex family was built for technical documentation, which is exactly this product's register: legible, unpretentious, and comfortable holding dense data.

Spacing, radius, shadow, icons

SystemRule
Spacing4px base unit, scale 4/8/12/16/24/32/48/64. Table and form density stays tight (8–12px); page-level rhythm stays generous (32–48px) so dense data doesn't read as clutter.
RadiusSmall and consistent — 3–6px. This is an instrument, not a consumer app; large rounded corners read as playful and undercut the evidentiary tone. Map controls and modals share the same 6px ceiling.
ShadowReserved for things that float above content — modals, dropdowns, the map's floating panel. Flat elsewhere. No shadow on static cards; a table row hover uses a background shift, never a shadow.
IconographyA single-weight line icon set, geospatial and technical in vocabulary (pin, polygon, layers, ruler, compass, cloud-cover, confidence-tier). Icons never carry meaning alone — always paired with a label or used alongside a semantic color, never as the sole signal.

08

Layout System

How the application holds itself together
Sidebar

Fixed, collapsible to icon-only rail. Persistent across the whole app so a 40-site detour never loses the analyst's place in the portfolio.

Header

Slim utility bar: global search, job status indicator, notifications, tenant switcher. Never carries primary navigation — that stays in the sidebar.

Content width

Tables and maps run full-bleed within the content area; text-heavy panels (reason lists, reports) cap at ~72ch for legibility.

Map + panel split

The core interaction pattern of the product. Map left/center, evidence panel right, resizable. On any screen with geography, this split is the default, not an alternate view.

Sticky elements

Table header, the four-fact verdict summary on a long assessment page, and the job-progress indicator stay pinned — everything else scrolls freely.

Density

Dense by default in tables and lists (portfolio scale demands it); spacious in the assessment detail view, where a single verdict deserves room to be read, not scanned.

This layout is appropriate because the product's two fundamental activities — scanning many sites and examining one site's evidence — are structurally different tasks that should not share one layout template. The shell stays constant; the content area switches modes.

Information Hierarchy per Screen Type

What dominates, what recedes
Assessment detail

Site name + type → Verdict + capacity (dominant) → confidence + binding constraint → reason list → map/boundary preview → panorama → generation P50/P90 → ML comparison (recedes) → provenance metadata (recedes furthest)

Portfolio table row

Verdict chip (dominant, leftmost) → site name → capacity → confidence → binding constraint → last updated (recedes) → row-hover actions (recedes furthest)

Portfolio overview

Ranked shortlist (dominant) → saturation summary (how much of the portfolio is assessed) → active job status → recent verdict changes (recedes) → quick filters

Boundary review

Cropped imagery + suggested boundary (dominant) → provider original (adjacent, equal weight — never subordinate) → obstruction notes → accept/reject (always visible, never scrolled past)

The rule that holds across all four: the thing a user must read to make a decision dominates; the thing that explains or justifies that decision recedes one step; raw provenance recedes furthest and is never load-bearing for the first read.

Accessibility

Risks specific to this product, not a generic checklist
  • Map-based interaction is the biggest risk. Boundary drawing on a map is inherently pointer-driven. Every geo-selection flow needs a keyboard/non-pointer fallback: address search → geocode, or coordinate/GeoJSON paste, so a screen-reader or motor-impaired user is never blocked from creating a site.
  • Color-only verdict communication is explicitly disallowed by the product's own logic (INSUFFICIENT_DATA must never read as a low score) — every verdict chip pairs color with text and, ideally, a distinct icon shape per verdict.
  • Data tables at portfolio scale need real header associations (scope, not just visual styling) so a screen reader announces "capacity, 2,340 kilowatts DC" rather than a bare number.
  • Contrast on semantic chips — amber/green/red on a warm paper background must be checked against WCAG AA independently of the brand palette's own contrast, since status colors are lower-chroma by design.
  • Touch targets for boundary-vertex editing are small by nature (map precision) — provide a zoom-assisted or numeric-nudge alternative for anyone who can't place a vertex precisely with touch or mouse.
  • Form accessibility for OCR-assisted fields (USN) — the extracted value and the confirm step must both be reachable and clearly labelled by keyboard, since this is a "must confirm before store" flow by requirement (USN‑02/03).
  • Error communication for constraint/geometry rejections should read as plain language ("boundary lies outside the plausible area for a rooftop site"), not internal codes.

Responsive Design

Two real usage contexts, not one shrinking layout

This product has two genuinely different device stories, not a single desktop app that degrades: an analyst workstation context (desktop/laptop, portfolio scale) and a field capture context (tablet/phone, single site, often outdoors).

BreakpointWhat changes
Desktop (≥1200px)Full sidebar, map+panel 50/50 split, dense multi-column tables.
Laptop (900–1199px)Sidebar collapses to icon rail by default; map+panel split narrows, panel becomes a slide-over on demand.
Tablet (640–899px)Map and panel become tabs instead of a split; portfolio tables shift from columns to stacked cards carrying the same four-fact hierarchy.
Mobile (<640px)Full analyst workflows are not optimised here — this width targets the field-capture flow specifically: boundary draw, USN capture, survey reconciliation. Navigation collapses to a bottom bar of 3–4 field-relevant actions.

Screens requiring special mobile UX

  • Boundary Draw / Edit — needs pinch-zoom-aware vertex placement and a large, unambiguous "close polygon" affordance.
  • USN Capture — camera capture for bill/payment-proof OCR is a first-class mobile flow, not a desktop upload dialog shrunk down.
  • Field reconciliation — a deliberately minimal single-site view: what the model predicted vs. what to measure, nothing else competing for attention outdoors, possibly in bright sunlight (high-contrast mode matters here specifically).

Portfolio ranking, bulk import and the ML/calibration admin surfaces are intentionally not designed mobile-first — they assume desk-based, sustained analytical work, and forcing them into a phone layout would cost more clarity than it returns.

Premium UX Principles

What "excellent" means for this specific product
Clarity over decoration

A tender committee will screenshot a verdict card into a government memo. It must read correctly at low resolution, in grayscale print, with zero context — verdict, number, confidence, constraint, nothing extraneous.

Speed as trust

The cache (§9.14) makes most repeat selections instant. The UI should make that speed visible and celebrated, not hidden — instant is a feature the product actually earned.

Predictability

The same four facts, the same order, on every surface — table row, card, map pin, PDF. A user should never have to re-learn where "confidence" lives.

Honest empty & error states

INSUFFICIENT_DATA is designed with the same care as SUITABLE — it is a legitimate, common, non-alarming outcome, never a broken-looking dead end.

Efficient bulk workflows

Boundary review at portfolio scale needs a keyboard-driven accept/reject rhythm (like a triage inbox), not a modal reopened per site.

Purposeful motion

Motion only where it carries meaning: a job's progress count ticking up, a map feature easing into view on selection, a verdict chip's subtle settle on load. Nothing decorative.

Deliberately avoided

Gradients and glassmorphism (this is a survey instrument, not a landing page); dashboard-template card sprawl (every card must justify its presence against the four-fact hierarchy); heavy shadows and oversized radii (undercuts the precision the product is selling); decorative icons without labels (a misread icon on a capacity figure is a real-world costly mistake); animated counters or celebratory confetti on a verdict (a NOT_SUITABLE result is not a failure state to apologise for, and a SUITABLE one is not a game win).

14

Signature UX/UI

What makes this feel like this product, not another SaaS shell
The Binding Constraint Spotlight

Most tools would show a capacity number. This one always shows the one thing stopping it from being higher, in the same visual weight as the number itself. It reframes every assessment from "here's a fact" to "here's what to do next" — survey the transformer, check the ownership record, request the exclusion review.

The Cache-Aware Result

A second selection of an already-analysed water body returns instantly, and the product says so plainly: "Already analysed 12 Aug 2026 · reused, zero new calls." Most products hide caching as an invisible optimisation; here it's evidence the platform gets smarter and cheaper with every user, worth surfacing as a feature.

Numeric Verdict + Panorama, Paired

The 3D panorama isn't a gimmick screen — it sits directly beside the capacity figure at the moment a bid team needs to sign off. Seeing the roof or the water surface next to the number is what makes a portfolio decision feel safe to make quickly.

Confidence as a First-Class Language

Not a tooltip afterthought — a consistent tiered indicator that appears everywhere a number does, so a user can triage a thousand-row portfolio by trustworthiness as easily as by size.

The Seasonal Spread Chart

For floating solar, min/mean/max water spread plus coefficient of variation is a genuinely novel data product nobody else surfaces (§13.3 of the source). A small, honest sparkline-style chart showing the driest month driving the boundary — with CV as an explicit ranking dimension — turns an obscure hydrology detail into the product's most defensible differentiator.

Boundary Provenance, Always Visible

Every geometry on screen carries a quiet, permanent tag — source, imagery date, confidence — the same discipline a surveyor's own field notes would carry. It's not a feature to discover; it's ambient, and it's what makes the product feel like an instrument rather than a guess.

None of these are gimmicks: each one exists because a specific requirement in the source document (CACHE‑05, VIZ, FIT‑04/05, the water-index seasonal profile, GEO‑04/09) already demands the underlying data — the design work is making that data legible instead of burying it.

Prioritising the Design Work

Aligned to the engine's own build order (§15 of the source document)
Phase 1 — Core UX

Site creation (address/pin + manual draw), single-site assessment view, portfolio list + map, batch import + job status. The product must be usable for one site and for a few hundred before anything else matters.

Phase 2 — Core UI

The design system: verdict chip, confidence meter, binding-constraint tag, data table, map+panel split, form-from-schema pattern. Built once, reused everywhere from here on.

Phase 3 — Secondary Screens

Boundary review (VIS), panorama viewer (VIZ), ML score panel, USN capture, calibration report, composite site builder, disclosure report.

Phase 4 — Polish

Motion on job progress and selection states; every empty/error/insufficient-data state individually designed; accessibility pass on map interactions; responsive field-capture mode; cross-surface consistency audit.

16

Final Design Blueprint

The single reference for everything that follows

Product UX

Six personas around one engine; the shared job is spending a limited survey/decision budget on the right subset of sites. Hierarchy: Engine → Site → Analysis → Assessment → Portfolio. Four facts — verdict, capacity, confidence, binding constraint — anchor every surface, from a table row to a PDF export.

UI

Technical, calm, evidentiary. Paper-neutral ground, one warm accent (solar amber) kept apart from a cooler structural blue used for wayfinding, and a semantic verdict palette kept apart from both. IBM Plex Sans for interface, IBM Plex Mono wherever numbers must be trusted. Small consistent radii, hairline structure, shadow reserved for floating layers only.

Screens

~28 screens across Portfolio, Sites, Boundary Capture, Imports/Jobs, Reports and Settings. MVP is the nine Phase‑1 screens; secondary screens carry the product's real differentiation (panorama, seasonal spread, cache provenance); the boundary editor and portfolio comparison view are the most complex builds.

UX Quality

Accessible non-pointer paths into every geo-selection flow; color never the sole signal on a verdict; states designed for insufficient-data and cache-hit as first-class outcomes, not edge cases; two distinct responsive stories (analyst desktop, field mobile) rather than one shrinking layout; motion used only where it carries meaning.

Design order

  1. Design foundationColor, type, spacing, radius, elevation, icon set — locked before any screen.
  2. Navigation shellSidebar, header, map+panel split pattern.
  3. Core component setVerdict chip, confidence meter, binding-constraint tag, data table.
  4. Site creation flowAddress/pin entry, manual draw, provider chain resolution feedback.
  5. Single-site assessment viewThe four-fact hierarchy, reason list, ceiling ledger.
  6. Portfolio list & mapThe home surface most personas open first.
  7. Bulk import & batch job statusAsync at portfolio scale, the RESCO/state-agency critical path.
  8. Boundary review & USN captureThe judged, human-in-the-loop screens.
  9. Panorama, seasonal chart, ML panelThe signature, differentiating surfaces.
  10. Reports & settingsDisclosure export, calibration, jurisdiction packs, API keys.
  11. Empty / loading / error / insufficient-data statesApplied to every screen built so far.
  12. Responsive & field-capture pass, then final polishMotion, accessibility audit, cross-surface consistency.
Governing question

For every screen from here forward: who is using it, what are they trying to accomplish, what information do they need to trust the answer, what should they do next — and how do we make that faster and clearer than the spreadsheet or the site visit it's replacing.
